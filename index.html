THE PV/FV CONNECTION

The strike is future money.
Discount it back to today.

You already know that money paid later has a present value. Black–Scholes uses the same idea.

Strike paid at expiry · FV$100.00
Discount factor0.951229
Value of that amount today · PV$95.12
PV = FV × e−rT= 100 × e−0.05 × 1 = 95.12

At 5% for 1 year, $95.12 today grows to $100.00 at expiry. That is why the formula uses the discounted strike, not the full future amount.

5% continuous is equivalent to 5.1271% effective annually.

With annual effective interest i, you learned PV = FV / (1 + i)T. Here 1 + i = er, so the two discount factors match. If given an effective rate, convert it using r = ln(1 + i).

See how today’s option prices are calculated +

CALL

C = S N(d₁) − PV(K) N(d₂)

PUT

P = PV(K) N(−d₂) − S N(−d₁)

d₁ = [ln(S/K) + (r + σ²/2)T] / (σ√T)   ·   d₂ = d₁ − σ√T

d₁ = 0.3500 · d₂ = 0.1500

N is the standard normal cumulative distribution function: it converts a d value into a number from 0 to 1. These terms account for uncertain future stock prices; discounting alone cannot price the option.

The model discounts an average future payoff under risk-neutral pricing, a no-arbitrage way of valuing risk. It does not use your forecast of the stock’s expected return or promise a risk-free option payoff.

Call − put = stock − PV(strike)10.45 − 5.57 = 100.00 − 95.12 = 4.88

This is put–call parity, a consistency check for matching European options.

Formula reference: Columbia University · The Black-Scholes Model

Model scope: European options (exercise at expiry), no dividends, constant volatility and risk-free rate, and frictionless trading. Market prices and American option values can differ.
